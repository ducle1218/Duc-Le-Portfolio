Putt-Putt Boat: Straight-Line Redesign

An individually redesigned candle-powered jet boat with an offset twin-straw exhaust, built to fix the class's baseline boat drifting into the test gutter's sidewalls.

≈58% Shorter Travel Time 1.6" Avg. Displacement (2.75" limit) 5/5 Criteria & Constraints Met

PROBLEM STATEMENT

The class's original candle-powered putt-putt boat used steam-jet propulsion but didn't move straight, repeatedly drifting sideways into the walls of the test gutter instead of holding a line.

CRITERIA & CONSTRAINTS

  • Reach the end in ≥120% of baseline speed
  • Displace < 1"/ft from a straight line
  • ≤10 added steps to the manufacturing plan
  • Total weight ≤ 25 g
  • Attachment part is renewable material

Engineering Notebook, March 20 – May 15, 2023

From a Wrong Guess to a Straight Line

March 20 — 21

First Hypothesis, Then Patent Research

Started from a guess: that candle smoke was being redirected through an angled pipe, and the pipe's angle gave the boat enough vertical vibration on the water to make the "putt-putt" sound. Reading the original patent afterward forced a check of that theory against the real mechanism.

March 22 — Revised Explanation

Correcting the Propulsion Model

After the patent, the notebook entry was rewritten: the candle boils water in the boiler, pressure builds until it forces water out through the exhaust straws in a jet flow, then the resulting low pressure pulls water back in through a weaker sink flow — the repeating cycle is what drives the boat and produces the "putt-putt" sound. The same session drafted safety guidelines specific to the build, including watching each demonstrated cutting step closely and keeping the workspace clear so tools aren't hidden under scrap material.

March 23 — 27

Preconstruction Planning & a Quality-Control Case Study

Reading the manufacturing plan ahead of the build flagged the boiler as the most likely point of failure and identified ten separate steps that carried real tolerances. A Newsela article on a lithium-ion battery fire reinforced why that mattered: catching a bad component during quality control, before final assembly, is what keeps a manufacturing defect from becoming a field failure.

April 26 — Observation Day

Watching the Baseline Boats Fail

Once the class's baseline boats were running, the pattern was obvious: they moved forward reliably but kept ramming into the sidewalls of the gutter. That observation, alongside a five-item list of other candidate problems (drift, an overly long build process, low speed, slow startup, no steerability), narrowed to one measurable problem statement and the criteria and constraints listed above.

April 28 — May 3

Brainstorming, Scoring, and Drawing the Fix

Coursework on the Otto-cycle heat engine and on jet vs. sink flow directly informed the fix: offsetting a pair of exhaust straws so their jet flow was symmetric about the boat's centerline would cancel the sideways component that was pushing the baseline boat into the wall. That idea was sketched into three candidate solutions and scored against the graded criteria, then checked against the two hard constraints separately.

Manufacturing Plan

Twin Straws, Angled 10° Down

The attachment is two 6"-long paper straws (0.25" diameter), each glued 1" from the boat's existing center exhaust straw and angled 10° downward. A loose wooden stick was clamped between the two new straws to hold that spacing and angle while the adhesive set, then pulled out once it dried — a jig made from scrap instead of a fabricated part, which kept the added step count low.

Materials

  • 2× paper straws, 0.25" diameter
  • Adhesive
  • Wooden spacer stick (removed after cure)

Build Steps

  1. Cut both straws to 6"
  2. Position each 1" from the center straw
  3. Adhere, repeating on both sides
  4. Set a wooden stick between the straws for spacing
  5. Glue the straws to the hull
  6. Remove the stick once cured

Mistakes & What They Taught Me

Wrong First Hypothesis

The initial write-up attributed propulsion to redirected smoke through an angled pipe. Reading the actual patent showed it was a pressure-driven jet/sink flow cycle instead — a reminder to check an early explanation against a primary source before designing around it.

Constraints Aren't Just Another Score

Solution 1 scored well on displacement control but failed the 25 g weight constraint outright. Scoring criteria and constraints separately kept an overweight design from winning on points alone.

Plan for Tolerances Before Building

Flagging which of the ten manufacturing steps carried real tolerances before construction started — rather than discovering it mid-build — made the boiler, the hardest single part, easier to get right the first time.

Conclusion & Next Steps

The redesigned boat met every criterion and constraint, reducing travel time by roughly 58% from the original 33.07 seconds while staying well inside the allowed displacement — averaging 1.6" against a 2.75" limit over the 33" gutter. Proposed next steps are small wooden fins beneath the hull and dampeners near the boiler, aimed at pushing the displacement margin even further.
