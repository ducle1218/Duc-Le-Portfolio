Tower Design Project

An 11" balsa wood compression tower, designed and built with partner Gabriel Falcone under instructors Kathleen Dunlevy and Gould Yazmin.

1,200:1 Strength-to-Weight Withstood 45 lbs 3/4 Specs Met

PROBLEM STATEMENT

Buildings constructed out of balsa wood buckle and fail structurally under compression. The challenge was to design a tower that resists that buckling while staying lightweight and buildable by hand.

SPECIFICATIONS

  • Deflect < 1/4" under 100 N
  • Height between 10.75"–11.25"
  • Footprint within a 3.5" x 3.5" square
  • Constructed entirely of balsa wood

Engineering Notebook, Jan 8 – Jan 22, 2024

Process, Mistakes & What I Learned

Every stumble below — a lost CAD file, a mismeasured stick, an under-spec test result — fed directly into the next decision. The notebook trail is kept honest on purpose.

January 8 — Project Design

Brainstorm, Criteria & Constraints

Sketched a wide range of tower shapes — pyramids, cylinders, trusses, hexagons — then wrote the criteria and constraints before doing any online research. A design found during research stood out for using a high density of support beams, and its core idea got folded directly into the sketches: more beams distribute compressive force more evenly through the structure.

January 9 — Project Proposal

Researching Tension/Compression Cores

Researched glulam and cross-laminated timber, then honed in on a specific structural principle: attaching a side column to a central core so that when the building bends, one column goes into tension while the opposite one goes into compression. That pairing resists side-loading far better than a single column alone, and it became the reasoning behind stacking layers of beams rather than using solid walls. Finished writing the formal specifications the same day.

January 10 — Solution Development

Three Concepts & a Decision Matrix

ENGINEERING NOTEBOOK PAGE #3
Dimensioned top, front, and layer views for the Double Square, Pyramid+Cube, and Cylindrical concepts.

Sketched and dimensioned the top three candidate solutions — a Double Square design (stacked, alternating 45°-rotated squares of beams), a Pyramid Plus Cube design, and a Cylindrical trussed design — then scored each against the specs in a decision matrix. The Double Square design won on strength: its dense beam count distributes compressive load most evenly and scored highest overall (8/8), while the Pyramid design lost points for a hollow, bend-prone interior and the Cylindrical design lost points for relying on large singular beams with no redundancy if one failed.

Decision Matrix Result: Double Square 8/8 · Cylindrical 7/8 · Pyramid + Cube 6/8.
January 11 — CAD Progress

Mistake: Rebuilding the CAD File From Scratch

Gabriel was absent and had the only copy of the CAD file, so the AutoCAD model had to be rebuilt from zero to keep the project moving. Redrew the front and top views with all dimensions labeled, exported the drawing to PDF, and started gathering and cutting the first pieces of balsa wood before the period ended.

Lesson learned: shared files need a shared, accessible location (cloud drive) from day one — a single local copy is a single point of failure for the whole team's schedule.

January 12 & 16 — Building Progress

Mistake: Forgetting to Account for Stick Thickness

ENGINEERING NOTEBOOK PAGE #5
The full cut list: 8 long support beams, plus dozens of small square-frame pieces, before the re-cut correction.

Cut all 8 support beams (11") and both sets of square beams (32 at 2⅜" and 32 at 2 3/16") on the 12th, but the whole batch of 2⅜" pieces had to be redone — the measurement didn't account for the ⅛" thickness of the wood itself, so the assembled squares came out oversized. On the 16th, all 32 pieces were re-cut down to 2 3/16" to correct for that overlap, then glued into square frames.

Lesson learned: when parts overlap at a joint, the material's own thickness has to be subtracted from the nominal dimension — measuring the finished assembly, not just each stick in isolation, would have caught this before any cutting started.
January 17 — Building Progress

Final Assembly & Report Cleanup

Gabriel handled the physical construction of the tower — stacking all 8 layers, rotating each 45° from the one below, and hot-gluing the 8 long support beams to the corners — while the build reached about 90% complete. In parallel, the earlier proposal and solution development write-ups were revised to fix mistakes flagged in review.

January 18 — Building Testing + Result

Compression Test on the Structures Testing Instrument

Construction finished at the start of class and the tower went straight onto the Structures Testing Instrument for a compression test. It began visibly bending at just 15 lbs (67 N) — short of the 100 N no-bend specification — then started twisting clockwise past 20 lbs as one support beam cracked at the 2nd layer, followed by more beams cracking at the same layer. The tower ultimately held 45 lbs of compressive force before failing, at a measured weight of only 0.0375 lbs — a strength-to-weight ratio over 1,200:1. When the load was removed, the structure untwisted counterclockwise and largely returned to its original shape, and post-test measurements confirmed it still met the height (11"), footprint, and all-balsa-wood specifications.

Result: 3 of 4 specifications met (height, footprint, material); the 100 N no-bend spec was not met — first visible bending occurred at 67 N.

Mistakes & What They Taught Me

File Ownership

Losing access to the only copy of the CAD file when a partner was absent cost a full rebuild. Now defaults to shared cloud storage for any file a teammate also needs.

Tolerancing

Forgetting to subtract material thickness at an overlapping joint meant re-cutting 32 pieces. Dimensions now get checked against the assembled joint, not just the isolated part.

Torsional Bracing

The tower twisted under load and failed at its beam corners well below the 100 N target. Diagonal bracing connecting each layer — so twisting beams put diagonals into torsion instead — is the proposed fix for a next iteration.

Final Design Reference

The chosen Double Square design stacks 8 layers of two overlapping squares, each rotated 45° from the layer below, connected by 8 corner support beams 11" long. Each layer measures 3⅜" wide and ¼" thick, built from ⅛"-thick beams — 32 at 2 3/16" for the small squares and 32 at 2⅜" for the large squares — spaced 1 5/16" apart (1 3/16" for the top layer), all joined with hot glue.
